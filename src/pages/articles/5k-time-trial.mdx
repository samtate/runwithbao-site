Training works best at the right paces: easy runs that are properly easy, and hard sessions that are hard enough to count. But the right paces move as you get fitter, and guessing them is hard. Guess too quick and you'll spend weeks tired. Guess too slow and you'll stop improving.

A 5K time trial settles it. Run 5 km as well as you can on the day, and you know where you are.

## What it tells you

**Your training paces.** A 5K time is one of the best guides there is to how fast your easy runs, tempo runs and intervals should be. Coaches have used tables like Jack Daniels' for decades to turn one race time into a full set of paces.

<PaceLadder />

**A rough idea of longer races.** A 25-minute 5K is about a 52-minute 10K and a 1:55 half marathon on paper. That's only true if you've done the training for the longer distance: a 5K can't tell you whether your legs will last for 21 km.

**Whether it's working.** Seeing a time come down after six weeks of steady running does more for motivation than any number of easy runs logged.

What it can't tell you is much from one bad day. Wind, heat, a poor night's sleep or a hilly course can each cost you a minute. If you know why it went badly, don't chase the number. There'll be another one in a few weeks.

> **In Bao:** the plan's paces come from what you actually run, and a time trial counts in full, so a good one moves your paces straight away. It's read as run hard: rate it afterwards, and if you held back, a lower rating tells Bao.

## How often?

Every four to eight weeks is plenty. Any more often and you're spending effort on testing that could go on training, and there's not much change to see from one fortnight to the next.

The best time is when your legs are fresh, at the start of a block of training rather than at the end of a hard week.

> **In Bao:** race plans include a 5K time trial every four weeks, in the week straight after a lighter week. If Bao only has your word for how fast you are, the first one comes in your third week, so a guess at sign-up doesn't set a month of sessions. There are none in the taper or close to a B race. Keeping-fit plans have one every eight weeks.

## How to run one

1. **Arrive fresh.** No hard session in the two days before, and an easy day or a rest day before it.
2. **Warm up properly.** Ten minutes of easy jogging, then a few short, quicker bursts of 15 to 20 seconds.
3. **Start controlled.** The first kilometre shouldn't be your fastest. Most people go off too hard and pay for it from halfway.
4. **Keep the effort even.** It should feel hard but sustainable until the last kilometre, then empty what's left.
5. **Cool down.** Ten minutes of easy jogging or walking.
6. **Note the conditions.** Hot, windy, hilly? Write it down next to the time.

<Pull>Start a touch slower than you think you can. You'll finish faster.</Pull>

> **In Bao:** the session is a 10-minute warm-up, 5 km hard but even, and a 10-minute cool-down. There's no pace target: you run it by effort, and the result sets the paces. On a Leisurely plan, it asks for a hard, steady effort rather than flat out.

## parkrun makes a good time trial

parkrun is a free, timed 5K most Saturday mornings, and running with other people makes a hard effort much easier to hold. Courses vary, from flat tarmac to muddy hills, so compare your times on the same course rather than across different ones.

> **In Bao:** tell Bao you go to parkrun, and on a time-trial week the trial is Saturday's parkrun. Miss it, and the run can go to another day.

## If you're new to running

You don't need a time trial while you're building up to running 5K at all. Couch to 5K is about getting there; the first time you run 5 km without stopping is your first result. After that, a time trial every month or two is a good way to see how far you've come.
